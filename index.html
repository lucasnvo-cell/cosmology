Overview

For decades, the universe was largely inaccessible to us. We could see its light, but the deeper story — the first fraction of a second, the seeds of every galaxy, the invisible scaffolding of dark matter and dark energy — remained hidden.

A sequence of landmark NASA missions changed that. From the first all-sky maps of the cosmic microwave background to the breathtaking infrared vision of the James Webb Space Telescope, each mission tore back another layer of the unknown.

This website traces those missions, explains what they discovered, and asks what a new generation of probes could reveal about the earliest moments of existence.

Key NASA Missions

01

Cosmic Microwave Background

COBE

1989 – 1993

The Cosmic Background Explorer was the first satellite dedicated to measuring the CMB — the afterglow of the Big Bang — with precision. Its instruments confirmed that the CMB spectrum matches a nearly perfect blackbody at 2.725 K, a landmark verification of the Hot Big Bang model.

More strikingly, COBE detected tiny temperature anisotropies at the level of one part in 100,000. These minuscule ripples are the imprint of quantum fluctuations in the very early universe — the seeds from which all galaxies, clusters, and large-scale structure eventually grew. Principal investigators George Smoot and John Mather received the 2006 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work.

Nobel Prize 2006
02

Cosmic Microwave Background

WMAP

2001 – 2010

The Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe mapped the CMB with thirty-five times greater resolution than COBE. Its full-sky temperature maps allowed cosmologists to measure the geometry of the universe — finding it to be spatially flat to within 0.4% — and to precisely pin down the age of the universe at 13.77 billion years.

WMAP also constrained the cosmic composition: roughly 4.6% ordinary matter, 24% dark matter, and 71.4% dark energy. This snapshot transformed cosmology into a precision science, establishing the standard ΛCDM model that all subsequent work builds upon.

Age: 13.77 Gyr
03

Cosmic Microwave Background

Planck

2009 – 2013 (data through 2018)

ESA's Planck satellite, launched alongside close collaboration with NASA scientists, achieved sensitivity ten times greater than WMAP across nine frequency bands. Its final 2018 data release produced the most detailed map of the CMB ever made, resolving fluctuations at angular scales as small as 5 arcminutes.

Planck's power spectrum measurements refined the number of light neutrino species, tightened constraints on primordial gravitational wave amplitude (ruling out several inflation models), and deepened the so-called Hubble tension — a now-famous discrepancy between the expansion rate inferred from the early universe versus direct local measurements.

Best CMB map to date
04

Galaxy & Deep Field Observation

Hubble Space Telescope

1990 – present

Hubble's Ultra Deep Fields — long exposures of apparently empty patches of sky — revealed that the universe contains at least two trillion galaxies. Its observations of Type Ia supernovae in the 1990s, combined with ground-based surveys, provided the first direct evidence that cosmic expansion is accelerating, implying the existence of dark energy.

Over more than three decades, Hubble measured Cepheid variable distances across hundreds of galaxies to calibrate the Hubble constant H₀, contributing centrally to the ongoing tension with CMB-inferred values. Its legacy underpins nearly every branch of modern observational cosmology.

Dark energy discovery
05

Infrared Deep Field & Early Universe

James Webb Space Telescope

2021 – present

JWST is the most powerful space observatory ever built. Operating at infrared wavelengths from its station at L2, it can peer through dust and stretch its vision to galaxies formed just a few hundred million years after the Big Bang. Its first deep field images surpassed Hubble's in resolution and sensitivity within hours of release.

Early JWST results have already challenged prevailing galaxy formation models: massive, apparently mature galaxies appear at redshifts beyond z = 10, earlier than standard ΛCDM simulations predicted. JWST is also characterizing exoplanet atmospheres and probing interstellar chemistry — but its cosmological legacy may prove the most transformative of all.

z > 13 galaxies confirmed

Deep Dive

The Cosmic Microwave
Background

Simulated CMB temperature anisotropy map — hot (gold) / cold (blue)

The cosmic microwave background is light that has been traveling through the universe for 13.8 billion years. It was released at the moment of recombination, approximately 380,000 years after the Big Bang, when the universe had cooled enough for protons and electrons to combine into neutral hydrogen and the cosmos became transparent to radiation.

Before recombination, photons and baryons were coupled into a hot, opaque plasma. Gravity pulled matter together; radiation pressure pushed it apart. This competition set up acoustic oscillations — sound waves propagating through the plasma — whose imprints are frozen into the CMB as the temperature fluctuations we measure today.

The angular power spectrum of the CMB — how the intensity of fluctuations varies with angular scale — encodes nearly every key cosmological parameter. The positions and heights of the acoustic peaks tell us the total energy density, the curvature of space, the baryon fraction, and the dark matter abundance. The CMB is, in effect, a photograph of the universe as an infant, and missions like COBE, WMAP, and Planck have read that photograph with ever-increasing precision.

Looking Ahead

How Future Probes Should
Explore the Early Universe

The next generation of instruments should push past the surface of last scattering and probe epochs we have never directly observed. Here are the most promising directions for future early-universe exploration.

  • CMB-S4 Ground Array A network of high-sensitivity telescopes at the South Pole and the Chilean Atacama Desert, CMB-S4 would reach noise levels 10× below Planck in polarization. Its primary target is the B-mode polarization signature — a subtle swirling pattern imprinted on the CMB by primordial gravitational waves if inflation occurred. Detecting it would be the first direct observational evidence for inflation and would constrain the energy scale at which it happened.
  • LiteBIRD Satellite (JAXA/NASA) Scheduled for launch in the late 2020s, LiteBIRD will measure CMB polarization from space across fifteen frequency bands. Unlike ground observatories that are limited by atmospheric emission at certain frequencies, a satellite can achieve full-sky coverage critical to separating the inflation signal from galactic foreground contamination.
  • 21-cm Hydrogen Mapping The Epoch of Reionization — when the first stars ionized the neutral hydrogen that had existed since recombination — is currently almost entirely unobserved. Radio telescopes tuned to the redshifted 21-cm emission line of hydrogen could create three-dimensional maps of this era spanning redshifts z = 6 to 30. Future arrays like the Hydrogen Epoch of Reionization Array (HERA) and SKA-Low aim to do exactly this, extending our observational reach to within a few hundred million years of the Big Bang.
  • Space-Based Gravitational Wave Observatories The Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA), planned for 2035, could detect a stochastic background of gravitational waves produced in the early universe — from phase transitions, cosmic strings, or preheating after inflation. Unlike electromagnetic probes, gravitational waves pass through all matter unimpeded, giving a direct window into epochs before recombination, as far back as the electroweak transition at 10⁻¹² seconds after the Big Bang.
  • Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope NASA's upcoming wide-field infrared survey telescope will image billions of galaxies across cosmic history, mapping the large-scale structure of the universe with unprecedented statistical power. Its weak gravitational lensing and baryon acoustic oscillation surveys will constrain dark energy's equation of state and may help resolve the Hubble tension — one of the most pressing open questions in cosmology today.
"The universe is not required to be in perfect harmony with human ambition."

Each mission described here pushed the boundary of the observable universe outward — not in space, but in time. COBE confirmed the Big Bang; WMAP made cosmology a precision science; Planck mapped the fluctuations from which everything grew; Hubble revealed that expansion accelerates; and JWST is now finding galaxies that should not yet exist.

The pattern is consistent: every time we build an instrument sensitive enough to hear something new, the universe surprises us. The future missions outlined above are not incremental improvements — they are designed to peer into epochs and phenomena that have never been directly observed. Whether they confirm the standard model or overturn it entirely, the only certainty is that cosmology is nowhere near finished.
